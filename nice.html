A coffee house in Nice

Nice serves twelve million visitors a year and still can't make a decent coffee

One of the busiest cities in Europe, thirty thousand students, a business district growing to the west, and a coffee and lunch offer that guidebooks politely describe as underwhelming. Twelve million visitors only need to find us once. The neighbourhood will find us every week. We open early on coffee, earn our keep at lunch, and hold the terrace through the long summer evenings.

Prepared for Daniel Burgos, Kris Wallenta and Andre, Grupo Marea.
September 2026. Name to be agreed.
01

What is already spoken for, and what is not

Nice guards its favourites carefully, and most of the categories worth having already belong to somebody. Understanding which ones are genuinely closed is the first useful thing to know, because it saves a great deal of wasted effort later.

Socca has been settled since before the war. Chez Pipo has been firing it in wood ovens since 1923, Chez Theresa since 1925 in an oven built in 1867, and no newcomer is going to displace either of them. Fenocchio has been scooping ice cream on Place Rossetti since 1966 and now runs to something like a hundred flavours. La Merenda still has no telephone and does not need one. These are family businesses with a century of affection behind them, and they should be left alone.

A second group is more recent but no less occupied. Burgers belong to Bubu and Le Bavastro, brunch to Café Marché and Popote d'Ondine, which regularly has a queue at the door. Natural wine belongs to La Cave Du Cours and La Part des Anges. All of these could theoretically be challenged, but doing so would mean spending years building affection against people who already have it, which is a poor use of the group's time and money.

Coffee sits differently. The city has a handful of respected places, Cafés Indien, HUG, French Coffee Shop and Canopée among them, but no single address that everyone names. One published review of a shop that Google itself labels as specialty describes it as overpriced and unremarkable, and argues that coffee in Nice generally underdelivers for what it costs. That is a complaint sitting in public, in a category people already search for, with nobody answering it.

A city this size should have a coffee everyone agrees on, and it does not.

02

One room, changing shape as the day goes on

The idea is not a café with food added as an afterthought, nor a restaurant that happens to serve coffee in the morning. It is a single kitchen working to one standard, doing more or less elaborate things depending on the hour and the month.

The higher spend comes from the quality of what goes into the food, never from putting on a different sort of service in the evening. The same chefs, the same suppliers and the same sensibility carry from the first coffee to the last plate, so that somebody who eats lunch there on a Tuesday in February recognises the kitchen when they come back for dinner in July.

June to September, 120 days

The full house

Coffee and pastry from early. Lunch with the terrace at capacity. Aperitivo running into dinner, with the full brigade and a second service. This is where most of the year's revenue is made, and the room is built to take it.

October to May, 210 days

The core rhythm

Coffee and pastry from early, lunch service, and the door closes after it. A core team, no evening cover, and a business that runs on residents, students and people who work nearby rather than on visitors.

Flexing the day by season rather than trading the same hours all year is what makes the economics work. An evening brigade only goes on the payroll in the months when there are people to feed, and the weekday lunch trade carries the quieter part of the year on its own. The result is roughly €927,000 of revenue at around 16% EBE, which is better than either a lunch-only operation running all year or a full dinner operation doing the same.

The rent test

Daniel's rule is that a single good trading day ought to cover a month's rent, and it is a sound piece of discipline because it quietly enforces a rent burden of around five per cent of revenue. On a site at €2,019 a month, one peak day at a €22 ticket needs 92 transactions to clear it, which a busy terrace does before the middle of the afternoon. Across a whole season the site covers its annual rent many times over.

04

Where the customers come from

The strongest sites in Nice draw on more than one kind of customer, which is what allows them to trade in February as well as August. Visitors carry the summer, offices and the university carry the weekdays, and the people who live nearby build the habit that holds the rest of it together.

Visitors

15.23 million airport passengers, over 12 million tourists

More than half arrive from outside France, a higher share than before the pandemic, and those arriving by air spend upwards of €110 a day. Hotel occupancy reaches 85% between June and September.

Students

More than 30,000 students and 3,000 staff

Université Côte d'Azur runs five of its six main campuses in Nice. Valrose is among the best situated university campuses in France, sitting on the tram and a few minutes from Gare Nice-Ville.

Offices

22,000 jobs planned at Grand Arénas, 100,000 square metres at Nice La Plaine

The largest business district in the Alpes-Maritimes sits to the west, and the centre itself is thick with law firms, consultancies and medical practices working at street level.

Residents

Libération 12,289, Gambetta 9,400, Jean-Médecin 6,536, Carré d'Or 5,096

Libération sits above the national average on income and below it on unemployment, while in the Carré d'Or well over half of working residents are managers or professionals.

The bar is lower than it sounds. In the quieter months the model needs around 150 transactions a day, which at a fifteen in a thousand capture rate means ten thousand people walking past, an ordinary figure for a central Nice street during trading hours. Building the local habit needs roughly 3.4% of Libération's residents, or 8.2% of the Carré d'Or's, to come once or twice a week.

05

Being found

Almost everybody who visits Nice, and a good half of the people who live there, will at some point type a version of the same question into a phone. Most operators treat that as something to worry about once the doors are open. It seems more sensible to choose the questions we intend to answer before we choose the menu.

Breakfast is the most interesting of the four. Every guide to the city names a different set of places, and one or two resort to inventing sub-categories because no single address commands the answer. It is worth separating from brunch, which is thoroughly taken. Brunch is a weekend occasion, whereas breakfast is a weekday habit, and it is habit that keeps a room full through the winter.

Working from a café barely exists as Nice writing at all, while Paris supports several dedicated guides to the subject, and Nice has thirty thousand students and a growing office population who need somewhere to sit. Iced coffee has no Nice answer whatsoever, in a city that is warm for five months of the year.

Nobody out-heritages Nice, but there is a good deal of room to be more modern than it currently is.

All four of those questions are answered by the same room, in the same hours, with the same product. Coffee is the front door, breakfast is the reason to return tomorrow, working is the reason to stay two hours, and iced coffee is the thing that leaves the building in somebody's hand. Every one of them fills the lunch tables.

How they get won

Local ranking runs on how recently and how consistently people have written about a place, and on details that can be verified rather than asserted. That makes it a matter of running the business well rather than of marketing it hard. Naming the roaster and the origin, and being straightforward about who is making the coffee, gives people something concrete to repeat. A deliberate programme of asking for reviews in the first ninety days, built into how the room is run rather than bolted on afterwards, does most of the rest. Recognition does more than either, because somebody who is greeted by name and handed their usual will write about it, and a well served stranger usually will not.

There is also an advantage in simply staying open. A great many independent places in Nice close or cut back hard through the winter, and their standing in local search weakens a little every year as a result. A business whose weekday trade comes from residents, students and nearby offices can keep going through the quiet months on its own demand, and the gap widens slightly with every season.

06

The room and the welcome

Mexican food is already served in Nice and there is no need to add to it. What the city is short of is not a cuisine but a manner of looking after people, and that is the thing this group actually has to bring.

The published complaint about coffee in Nice is that it is expensive and unremarkable, which is as much a judgement about how customers are treated as about what is in the cup. Latin American hospitality is warmer by habit than French café convention tends to be, it cannot be copied by the place two doors down, and it costs nothing in service time. Everything else that makes the room distinctive has to sit alongside the transaction rather than inside it, because anything that slows the counter down works against the format.

A second counter for regulars

Somewhere for standing orders and familiar faces to be handed a coffee without queueing behind a visitor still reading the menu. It serves the locals properly, and it has the useful side effect of showing every visitor that the place is genuinely somebody's local rather than a room arranged for tourists.

Something to take away

Canned cold brew and bottled filter from a fridge by the door. Full price, no time at the machine, the best margin in the building, and the name travels out into the city in somebody's hand. Sold in returnable glass, every one of them carries a small reason to come back.

A short menu, properly done

Restraint reads as confidence, and it is faster to cook, quicker to order from and less wasteful besides. In a market whose complaint is that things are unremarkable, six dishes done beautifully will beat twenty done adequately.

The terrace treated as the main room

Most places treat the terrace as spillover. It is worth treating it as the point: shade, comfortable chairs and an hour of sitting without being moved on. The counter earns its money on speed and the terrace earns its money on the time people are willing to spend there.

The thing that compounds over time is recognition. Knowing somebody's order, their name and what happened to them last week costs nothing, cannot be bought by a competitor and is the actual reason a person comes four times a week rather than twice a month. The difficulty is that it usually lives in one barista's memory and leaves when they do, so it needs building into how the room is run: named tabs, a note of the usual order, and a handover between shifts that covers people rather than only stock.

How a visitor actually experiences it

A visitor to Nice stays about three nights, which means they can plausibly come back three or four times in a single trip. We are not trying to win them for life. We are trying to become their place for that trip, and almost everything that gets in the way is free to fix.

1

The night before

Thinking: I get six meals on this trip and I do not want to waste one.

They cannot tell recency from volume. A place with four hundred reviews averaging 4.3 may have been coasting since 2019. Google offers no way to tell, so they default to whichever has the most photographs, usually the most touristed and least good.

What we do

A steady flow of reviews from the last thirty days proves the place is good now, which a high average cannot. Photographs show the room with people in it at eight on a Tuesday rather than a styled flat lay.

2

The decision

Thinking: They all say the same things. How do I choose?

Every café in Nice claims artisanal, fait maison, specialty. The words have stopped carrying information, so visitors discount all of them equally.

What we do

Say things that can be checked. Name the roaster, the origin, who is behind the counter. Open every day, half seven to six, all year, does more work than any adjective because it is falsifiable.

3

Arrival on the street

Thinking: Is this for me or for them? Am I about to be overcharged?

French shopfronts give almost no read. A beloved local institution and a tourist trap can look identical, and Nice has enough of the latter that visitors arrive primed for it.

What we do

Prices go on a board outside, legibly, including the terrace. And what they see through the glass matters: people at the counter being handed coffees they did not have to order answers the question without a word.

4

The threshold

Thinking: I do not know the rules here.

The highest drop-off point in the whole journey. French café convention is unwritten and varies by place. Getting it wrong in front of a full room is a small public humiliation, and people leave rather than risk it. Nobody reports this, because they never became a customer.

What we do

Someone acknowledges them within three seconds. Not seats them, just makes eye contact and says how it works. Sit anywhere, we come to you. Five words that dissolve the entire problem, and the one behaviour in the room worth the most.

5

The language moment

Thinking: My French is bad and I am about to be an inconvenience.

The visitor opens in bad French, the server switches to English, and the visitor reads that switch as a correction. It is meant as helpfulness and lands as judgement.

What we do

Let them finish in French, answer in French, let them choose to switch. If they visibly struggle, switch without commenting on it. Never announce that we speak English, which tells them their attempt failed.

6

The menu

Thinking: I cannot tell what is good, what it is, or how long it takes.

Under that load people order the safest recognisable item, usually the cheapest and least interesting. They have a worse meal and we take less money, both caused by the same failure.

What we do

A short menu halves the load. A time marker against each item, five minutes for coffee, twenty for a plate, forty for the full lunch. One line of English beneath each dish describing rather than translating, in smaller type, so a French reader ignores it and an English reader is rescued by it.

7

Ordering

Thinking: I would ask what is good, but not in French.

They want a recommendation and asking is effortful, so most do not. They order without the thing that would have made the meal memorable.

What we do

Offer before being asked. The thing to have this week is X, delivered unprompted at every table, as a scripted behaviour rather than a personality trait. It raises the ticket and gives them something to tell people about.

8

The wait

Thinking: I have a museum booking. Is this going to take an hour?

Nobody tells them. Uncertainty about duration is why time-pressed visitors take a sandwich instead of sitting down, and why the middle of the day underperforms across tourist areas.

What we do

The menu already answered it, so the anxiety largely does not arise. Anything running late gets acknowledged before the customer notices. Told another five minutes, they stay comfortable. Left to guess, they start calculating.

9

During

Thinking: This is nice.

Nothing goes wrong, which is the problem. A pleasant, competent meal produces no memory and no review. Most places lose the trip here by being merely fine.

What we do

One thing per visit that was not transactional. Recognising them from yesterday. A word about where the coffee comes from. Being told what to do with their afternoon. It does not need to be the same gesture, it needs to be one.

10

Payment

Thinking: I am finished and now I am waiting.

The most complained about moment in French hospitality, landing at the worst point in the journey. The good feeling drains out while they try to catch an eye. It is also the last thing that happens, so it is disproportionately what they remember and write about.

What we do

Bring the bill unprompted once plates are cleared, assuming a visitor is in a hurry until proven otherwise. Offer paying at the counter when it arrives. Neither costs anything, and few places bother, which makes it ownable.

11

Leaving

Thinking: That was good. Where shall we go tomorrow?

Nothing brings them back. They had a good time and will now go elsewhere, because there is no reason not to. The trip is three days and we got one visit.

What we do

We open at half seven if you want coffee before the train. One sentence, said to every departing visitor, is the difference between one transaction and three. The canned coffee sits by the door for the same reason.

12

The review

Thinking: On the train home, writing while it is still warm.

Most places never ask, so only the annoyed and the delighted write anything, and the annoyed write faster.

What we do

Ask once, after the second visit rather than the first. Someone who came back has already decided they like it. What they write was determined twelve stages ago: the price was the same outside, they told me how long it would take, the locals were queueing, they brought the bill without being asked.

Not one of these needs capital. There is no line for any of it in the build cost. It is training, menu design, a board outside and a handful of sentences we decide to say. Each one is engineered to produce the review that wins the searches, which is why the welcome and the search strategy are the same piece of work approached from opposite ends.

One line is not worth trimming. Restaurant budgets rarely come apart on the large numbers; they come apart on the ones nobody counted. Three months of working capital alongside a spring opening is careful. Two months would not be, and no saving found elsewhere would be worth the risk of it.

Fonds de commerce in France change hands at somewhere between 60 and 150 per cent of average annual revenue. A business settled at €900,000 is therefore worth between €540,000 and €1.35 million as something that can be sold, and that value grows with every year of trading history, every review and every bit of ground held in local search.

Beyond the single room, this would be the group's first address in Europe. A Nice business that works becomes a template, a set of supplier relationships, a French operating structure and a credential in a market that takes hospitality seriously. Casa Kinta took Grupo Marea from one restaurant to a portfolio. This would take the portfolio across an ocean.

The early years pay in what the business is worth rather than in what it distributes, which is normal for hospitality and is the right trade when the asset appreciates alongside its reputation.

08

What would turn this into a decision

Four things, none of them expensive, and between them they close the remaining unknowns.

Counting the street

An hour at eight, an hour at one and an hour at seven, on a Tuesday and again on a Saturday, in three parts of the city with different characters. Two days of somebody's time, and the last real unknown in the model is closed.

Ticket and throughput from the kitchen

Real numbers from Daniel and Rodrigo for the coffee and lunch format, replacing sector averages with what the two of them already know from running rooms.

A shortlist held against the rent rule

A working kitchen, extraction already installed, a terrace, and a monthly rent that a single good day clears. Anything that fails that test comes off the list regardless of how the frontage looks.

Opening in April or May

A spring opening buys a full first season in which to build cash, reputation and reviews before meeting the first winter. It is worth more than the difference between two good properties.

Nice has the visitors, the students, the offices and the money. What it lacks is somewhere everyone agrees on for a coffee, for breakfast, or for sitting down to work, and one room could answer all three.
